Why the Boltzmann plot can fail, and what to do next (collisional–radiative modelling)

The Boltzmann plot assumes the emitting levels are populated according to a Boltzmann distribution, which requires electron collisions to dominate over radiative decay (local thermodynamic equilibrium, LTE). In atmospheric-pressure argon plasma jets nₑ is typically 10¹³–10¹⁶ cm⁻³, far below the McWhirter limit for full LTE (~10¹⁷ cm⁻³). The 4p (2p) levels are then populated by electron impact from the ground state and from the metastable 1s levels, and they are depopulated by radiation and quenching. The fitted "temperature" can then depend on which lines you choose.

Signs that LTE fails: a poor straight-line fit; 4p-only and 4p→5p slopes that disagree; lines from the same upper level that disagree (more often self-absorption or calibration error); or nₑ below the McWhirter criterion.

What to use instead: a collisional–radiative (CR) model of the Ar 1s/2p system, which predicts line ratios as a function of Tₑ, nₑ and metastable density. Examples: X.-M. Zhu and Y.-K. Pu, "A simple collisional–radiative model for low-temperature argon discharges with pressure ranging from 1 Pa to atmospheric pressure", J. Phys. D 43 015204 (2010); S. Siepa et al., "On the OES line-ratio technique in argon and argon-containing plasmas", J. Phys. D 47 445201 (2014). Students can compare measured ratios such as 750.4/751.5 or 696.5/750.4 with CR-model curves.

Model, assumptions and when to use massiveOES instead

The simulation treats the C³Πᵤ → B³Π_g (0,0) band as a Π–Π transition with unresolved triplet fine structure: P, Q and R branches with Hönl–London factors and rotational constants B′ = 1.8154 cm⁻¹ and B″ = 1.6285 cm⁻¹. Upper-level populations follow a Boltzmann distribution at Trot, and lines are convolved with a Gaussian instrument function. The band origin is pinned so the head falls at 337.13 nm (air), and a small shift is fitted.

This is adequate for teaching and for comparing conditions measured on the same instrument (typically within ±10–15% of a full model when the FWHM is ≥ 0.1 nm). For publication-quality temperatures, export the spectrum and fit it with massiveOES (Voráč et al., Masaryk University), which uses full line databases for N₂, OH and other molecules.

Physics caveats: Trot(N₂ C) ≈ Tgas only if the C state is created by electron impact from ground-state N₂ and lives long enough to thermalise. In argon, energy transfer from Ar metastables can overpopulate high rotational levels and inflate Trot. Compare with OH(A) where possible, and keep the fit window clear of the (1,1) head at 333.9 nm.

Parameters, sources and limits

Which Stark coefficient? Published values for Ar I 696.5 nm differ by about a factor of two at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³: Kunze eq. 10.26 gives 8.5 pm at 1 eV (6.6 pm at 2 eV); the linear default 11.2 pm; the Griem-formula fit used by Ananthanarasimhan, Gangwar et al., J. Appl. Phys. 129, 223301 (2021) gives ≈14.9 pm (Tₑ 1.7–2.4 eV). The table below lists nₑ for all of them so the spread is visible; always quote the source and Tₑ. For the full Griem form (Kunze 9.57/9.62), A ∝ nₑ1/4 and R = 8.34×10⁻⁵ Tₑ−1/2 nₑ[m⁻³]1/6; take w and A from Griem (1974) Table IV or the NIST line-broadening bibliography.

Linear default: wS = 0.0112 nm (FWHM at nₑ = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, Tₑ ≈ 10⁴ K) reproduces the published conversion of Lu et al., Sci. Rep. 4, 7488 (2014): WStark = 0.038 nm ↔ nₑ = 3.4×10¹⁶ cm⁻³. Check it against Konjević & Konjević, Spectrochim. Acta B 52, 2077 (1997) or Griem's tables before using results quantitatively; the electron-temperature dependence and the small ion-broadening term are neglected.

van der Waals (Lee et al. 1975; Konjević & Konjević 1997): α = 16.54×10⁻²⁵ cm³ (Ar), μ = 19.97 u, N0 = P/(kBTg). This is identical to C·P[atm]/Tg0.7 with C ≈ 1.54 nm·K0.7 (Yubero et al., as used in Atoms 5, 41, 2017), with ⟨R²⟩ = ⟨R²up⟩ − ⟨R²low⟩ from the Coulomb approximation (2p₂ upper, 1s₅ lower): ⟨R²⟩ ≈ 36.6 a₀², C ≈ 1.54 nm·K0.7, so WvdW ≈ 0.025 nm at 350 K. Resonance broadening is negligible because the lower level (1s₅) is metastable.

Detection limit: at 1 atm the van der Waals width (~0.02–0.03 nm) is much larger than the Stark width below ~10¹⁵ cm⁻³ (≤ 0.001 nm). Small errors in Tg or C then swamp the Stark part, and the tab reports nₑ as an upper limit. Use a spectrometer with WI ≲ 0.03 nm, and measure WI with a narrow lamp line near 696 nm at the same slit.
